## The problem

A conventional drone is tethered to its radio: a few kilometres of range, line of
sight, and a ground station that has to be physically nearby. We wanted the
opposite — a drone that registers itself with a server the moment it powers on, and
an operator who can be in a different city.

That turns a radio problem into a networking problem. Mobile networks jitter, drop
packets and change IP addresses mid-flight, and none of that is acceptable for a
control link. The interesting work was making a hostile transport behave well enough
to fly on.

  ### One link, two very different payloads

  Control traffic is tiny and cannot be late. Video is large and can tolerate a
  dropped frame. Pushing both down the same modem meant separating them early:
  MAVLink goes through a Go forwarder that speaks `gomavlib`, video goes into a
  GStreamer pipeline that never touches the control path.

  On the ground side a Go router fans MAVLink out to multiple endpoints — a
  QGroundControl instance, a web dashboard, a logging consumer — each authenticated
  by System ID, so adding an observer never costs the pilot anything.

  MediaMTX handles video redistribution, which meant one RTSP ingest could be served
  back as WebRTC for the browser and HLS for anything that could not do better.

  ### Getting under 300 ms

  Latency is where a connected drone either works or does not. Anything past roughly
  half a second and an operator starts over-correcting.

  The budget came apart into capture, encode, transport and decode. The wins were:

  - **Encode on the Pi's hardware H.264 path**, not in software — the single largest saving.
  - **Composite the two IMX219 cameras into one picture-in-picture stream** in GStreamer,
    so we paid for one encode and one connection instead of two.
  - **Tune MediaMTX for low latency over reliability** — small buffers, WebRTC to the
    browser instead of HLS, and no transcoding anywhere in the path.

  Measured end to end — lens to operator screen — the result held **under 300 ms** on a
  normal commercial 4G connection.

## Surviving a bad network

Mobile links do not fail cleanly; they degrade. The forwarder had to keep a session
alive through IP changes, brief blackouts and reordered packets.

- A **WireGuard tunnel** gives the drone a stable address even when the carrier
  reassigns its public IP mid-flight, so the router never sees the session move.
- **HMAC-based registration** against the fleet server ties each airframe to a UUID.
  A drone authenticates once at boot; the router refuses MAVLink from anything it
  cannot attribute.
- Choosing **TCP for control and UDP for video** matched each payload to what it
  actually needs. Combined with retry and reconnect logic in the forwarder, this cut
  effective packet loss on unstable links by around **5%**.

Auto-detecting the Pixhawk's System ID at startup removed a whole category of
misconfiguration — the drone tells the server who it is rather than being told.

## Finding the landing zone

The onboard vision task was landing zone detection: recognise the pad in the downward
camera and report its offset so the flight stack can correct.

It runs as template matching in OpenCV on the companion computer rather than a neural
network, which was the right call for the constraint — the pad is a known, high-contrast
marker, and template matching left the CPU budget free for encoding. In real outdoor
conditions, with changing light and altitude, it identified the zone correctly about
**80%** of the time.

## The ground station

Operators were switching between QGroundControl for flight and a separate browser tab
for video. I customised a QGroundControl build (Qt 6) to pull the RTSP stream, the
MAVLink authentication and the landing-pattern overlay into the same window. Removing
that context switch is where the roughly **40% improvement in operational efficiency**
came from — it is a workflow number, not a systems one, but it was the change operators
noticed most.

## What I worked on

This was a company R&D project with several people on it. My part was the connectivity
and streaming layer: the Go MAVLink forwarder and router, the GStreamer/MediaMTX video
path, the 4G module bring-up and WireGuard tunnelling, the landing zone detection, and
the QGroundControl customisation. The PX4 board support and the neural-network control
module were the wider team's work.
