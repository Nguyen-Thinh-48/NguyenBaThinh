## The problem

Customers ask about products on Messenger and in the comments under a Page post,
usually outside working hours. A cold enquiry by morning is normally a lost one.

A generic chatbot makes that worse rather than better. If it invents a price, a lead
time or a feature that does not exist, the brand pays for the mistake. So the
requirement was narrower than "add an LLM": **answer from the company's own documents,
or admit you do not know and hand the person to a human.**

## Why the webhook does not answer the question

This is the decision the rest of the system is built around, and it is the one that
took the longest to get right.

Facebook expects a `200` back from your webhook quickly. Retrieval plus a model call
is nowhere near quick enough. The naive workflow — receive, retrieve, generate, reply,
*then* respond — times out, and Facebook retries, and now the customer gets the same
answer three times.

So the webhook does almost nothing. It parses the event, drops obvious noise, writes
one row to a table with `Status: pending`, and returns. **A separate schedule trigger
polls that table every three seconds** and does the slow work outside the request.

  ### A row is a job

  Every message becomes a row whose `Status` walks a small state machine:
  `pending → processing → Done`, with `Error` and `skipped` as the other exits.

  That one column buys a lot:

  - **Crash safety.** A row stuck on `processing` is visible. Nothing is lost in memory.
  - **Idempotency.** A Facebook retry finds the message already recorded rather than
    generating a second answer.
  - **Back-pressure.** Messages are claimed one at a time, so a burst of traffic queues
    instead of firing twenty concurrent model calls.
  - **Observability.** "What happened to this customer's message?" is a table lookup,
    not a log grep.

  It is the same pattern as a job queue with a database backend. n8n does not ship one,
  so the Data Table became it.

  ### Retrieval is a tool, not a preprocessing step

  The knowledge base is wired to the agent as a **tool it can call**, not as text
  stapled to the front of the prompt. The agent decides a lookup is needed, issues the
  query, and reasons over what comes back. Questions that need no documents skip
  retrieval entirely.

  Three settings do most of the work:

  - **`top-k = 3`.** More chunks is not better. Past a handful, the relevant passage
    gets buried and answers drift toward whatever filler was retrieved alongside it.
  - **`temperature = 0.3`.** Support answers should be reproducible. Two customers
    asking the same question should get the same reply.
  - **A 400-token output cap**, backed by a length rule in the instructions. Nobody
    reads a nine-paragraph Messenger reply.

  The question and the documents go through the **same embedding model**. That is not
  an optimisation, it is a correctness requirement — vectors from two different models
  live in different spaces and their cosine distance means nothing.
</MediaSplit>

  ### Why 800 and 100

  **800 tokens** is roughly one coherent topic — a full procedure or a complete policy
  paragraph. Smaller chunks retrieve cleanly but arrive without the context that makes
  them usable; larger ones dilute the embedding until it stops matching anything
  specific.

  **100 tokens of overlap** exists because a splitter has no idea where an idea ends. It
  will happily cut a seven-step procedure between step four and step five. Overlap means
  the boundary appears in both chunks, so whichever one is retrieved still carries the
  seam.

  **The comparison step** is the unglamorous one that matters most. Without it, every
  trigger re-downloads and re-embeds the whole folder — burning embedding quota and
  filling the store with duplicate vectors that then compete with each other at query
  time. Only new and changed files go through.

  The person who owns the product information drops a file in a Drive folder. That is
  the entire publishing workflow, and it is why the knowledge base is still current.
</MediaSplit>

## Two agents, not one

Messenger and Page comments look similar and behave differently. A private message is a
conversation; a public comment is a reply visible to everyone else reading the post.

They run as **separate agents with separate memory tables**, keyed differently — a
Messenger session is the sender, a comment session is scoped to the comment thread — so
a public thread never inherits context from someone's private conversation, which would
be both wrong and a privacy problem.

Both share one instruction set with a strict precedence:

1. **Reply in the language the customer wrote in.** The knowledge base is in English;
   retrieved English text must never drag the reply into English. This needed explicit,
   repeated instruction — it is the rule models break most often.
2. **The retrieved chunks are the only source of truth.** Facts, step counts, button
   labels and policy terms come from the documents, in the documents' wording.
3. **Not in the chunks? Say so and escalate.** Never fill the gap.
4. **Answer length caps**, with numbered steps for procedures.
5. **Never disclose infrastructure**, internal identifiers, admin workflows or roadmap
   material, even when asked directly.

Rule 3 is the whole product. A support bot that says "I don't have that, here is who
does" is useful. One that guesses is a liability.

## Handing over to a human

A per-user toggle lives in its own table. A salesperson sends a command in the thread,
the bot goes quiet **for that customer only**, and everyone else keeps being served.
Switching it back is the opposite command.

Two filters keep the system from talking to itself:

- **Echo filtering.** The Page's own outgoing messages come back through the webhook.
  Without a filter the bot reads its own reply as a new customer message and answers it,
  forever.
- **Command filtering.** Operator commands are routed to the toggle instead of being
  treated as questions.

## When it breaks

Model calls fail. The agents retry, and a failure that survives the retry does not
disappear quietly: the row is marked `Error` and **a Telegram message arrives with the
customer's ID and what they asked**, so a human can pick it up while the enquiry is
still warm.

The failure mode I care about is not "the bot was wrong" — it is "nobody knew the bot
was wrong". Every exit from the state machine is recorded.

## Self-hosted, not n8n Cloud

Worth being precise about, because "built on n8n" usually means someone clicked
around n8n Cloud. This does not run there.

The whole stack — the **n8n instance itself** and **PostgreSQL with the PGVector
extension** — runs in **Docker Compose on a Hostinger VPS** that I set up and maintain.
I own the containers, the database, the backups and the upgrades.

That was a deliberate choice over the managed product:

- **Customer conversations stay on infrastructure the company controls.** For a brand
  handling enquiries and contact details, that is not a preference. On the hosted
  product, every message would transit someone else's tenant.
- **Cost is a fixed monthly server bill**, not per-execution pricing that scales with
  exactly the thing you are trying to encourage. A chatbot that succeeds gets more
  expensive on a metered plan.
- **No workflow-execution limits** to design around, and no waiting on a vendor to
  enable a node.
- **Backup, upgrades and scaling become ordinary ops problems** with ordinary answers,
  instead of tier limits.

The trade is real — nobody else is on call for that VPS, and a bad `docker compose`
day is mine to fix. At this workload it has been the right side of the trade, and
running it myself is where most of what I learned about the system came from.

## Understanding it from the bottom, not the docs

I could wire this together because I had already built the pieces by hand. The
[transformer and INT8 project](/projects/llm-from-scratch) is where I wrote the
tokenizer, the attention, the embedding table and the quantizer myself.

That is why the choices above are choices rather than defaults. Knowing that an
embedding is a learned vector and cosine similarity is just a normalised dot product is
what makes "use the same model for queries and documents" obviously mandatory instead of
a line in a tutorial. Knowing what a context window costs is what makes a 4-turn memory
and a 3-chunk retrieval a budget rather than a guess.

n8n is the fast way to ship it. Knowing what is underneath is what makes it debuggable
when it misbehaves.
